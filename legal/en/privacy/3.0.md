## 3. The principle the application is built on

Ripple Effect runs on your computer. Your stories, projects, boards, passages,
scripts and exports are files in folders you chose, and **the application does
not send them anywhere**. There is no background synchronisation and no
content analysis, and there are no usage statistics unless you switch them on
(§4.5).

**From version 1.5, the application makes no network request at all until you
have accepted the [End-User Licence Agreement](/eula/).** An earlier build
does not ask for acceptance and makes the update check in §4.1 on first
launch — a request that carries nothing about you. That is the only request
such a build makes unasked: the engine-fix check in §4.6 and the statistics in
§4.5 both arrive with 1.5 as well, so neither exists in a build that does not
ask.

After acceptance, **§4 is the complete list of what the application sends**,
and it is kept complete: a new request is described here, naming the release
that starts making it, before that release. What may be added is bounded by
the four rules in Article 7 of the End-User Licence Agreement — in short,
nothing you author and no identifier without your say, anything about you
only if you switch it on or answer yes, and anything else only to our own
site and carrying nothing about you. Signing in to the hosted service, if you
ever do, is covered separately in §8.

### 3.1 What the application reads on your computer — only if you allow it

Some of what the application can use is not a file you chose but a part of
your computer. From Ripple Effect 1.6 it can read three of them:

| | What reads it | Until you answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The microphone** | Dictation in the editor, while you are dictating | You are asked the first time |
| **The computer's location** | A story you play that changes with where you are | You are asked the first time |
| **The date and time** | A story you play that changes with the time of day | Allowed |

**Each is read only if you allow it, and only while something you started
needs it.** Your answers are in the application's settings, under
*Permissions*, and you can change any of them at any time; your operating
system may ask you as well, and its own privacy settings can refuse what the
application would allow. While anything is reading one of them, an indicator
on the application's bar says so and names what is reading it. The date and
time are allowed until you say otherwise because a story's clock tells it
nothing your computer has not already shown you; you can deny them like the
others.

**None of it leaves your computer, and none of it reaches us.** What the
microphone hears is turned into text on your computer, for the passage you are
dictating into, and neither the sound nor the text is sent anywhere. The model
that listens is downloaded once, when you ask for it (§4.8), and needs no
network after that. A story
receives the location and the time only if you allowed them, and only while
you are playing it; it keeps neither once you stop. The location is asked of
your operating system's own location service when a story that reads it
begins, at about the accuracy of a town, and is never turned into a place
name, which would mean asking somebody on the network. How that service
works out where your computer is — from nearby Wi-Fi networks, for instance
— is your operating system's processing, under its own vendor's notice, not
ours. A story played by an AI assistant you connected to the application is never
given the location. The session log (§6)
records that an answer was changed, never what was read.

A story exported from Ripple Effect and played inside somebody else's
software is that software's to answer for. The engine inside it reads nothing
of your computer by itself and is handed only what that software decides to
give it, under that publisher's own notice.

## 4. What the application sends

### 4.1 An update check — once per launch

The application asks `ripplefx.app` what the most recent release is. The
request carries **no identifier, no version number and no information about
you**; the comparison against your installed version happens on your machine.
As with any request over the internet, the server necessarily sees the
originating IP address and the connection's technical metadata.

*Purpose:* telling you that an update exists. *Legal basis:* legitimate
interest in the security and correct functioning of software already installed
(art. 6.1.f). *Retention:* we keep no record of individual requests. The
site's host, **Vercel**, logs each request it serves — when, the address it
came from, the path and the browser's description of itself — and we can read
that log for **one hour**, after which it is gone from our view. Vercel keeps
its own records of the traffic it carries under [its privacy
policy](https://vercel.com/legal/privacy-policy).

### 4.2 A list of contributors — when you open the About box

The application asks `ripplefx.app` for the people it credits — those who
work on the application, translate it and build its engine's bindings — so
the About box can show them; the site fetches those lists from GitHub itself
and keeps them for an hour, so your request never reaches GitHub.
The request carries the application's name and version as a `User-Agent` and
nothing about you. *Retention:* the host's request log, as in §4.1.

Versions before 1.5 asked GitHub's public API directly, with a read-only
access token that existed only to buy a rate limit; GitHub, as the operator of
that API, saw those requests' IP addresses under [its own privacy
statement](https://docs.github.com/site-policy/privacy-policies/github-general-privacy-statement).

**Their pictures are fetched from GitHub by your own machine.** The list the
site answers with carries each contributor's avatar address on
`avatars.githubusercontent.com`, and the application loads those images
directly rather than through `ripplefx.app` — so opening the About box does
make requests to GitHub, for the images although not for the list. One of
those addresses is compiled into the application, so the request is made even
on a launch where the list itself could not be fetched. GitHub sees the IP
address and the ordinary metadata of an image request, under its own privacy
statement above, and is named in the [sub-processors list](/subprocessors/) as
an independent controller for requests made to it. Nothing identifying you or
your installation goes with them, and no image is cached anywhere but in the
application's own memory for as long as the window is open.

*Legal basis:* legitimate interest in crediting contributors accurately (art.
6.1.f).

### 4.3 A crash report — only if you say so, and only for that report

This is the only thing the application sends that can contain anything of
yours, and §5 describes it in full.

**Consent is given per report.** After a crash, the next launch shows you a
dialog naming that crash, listing every file it would send with its size and a
description, and asking. Nothing is sent unless you answer *Send report*.
There is no setting that answers in advance, in either direction, and
declining is final for that report.

Before it asks, the application checks that the server it would send to can
be reached — a request to Supabase carrying nothing but the application's
publishable key — so that it never asks a question whose answer cannot be
acted on. If the server cannot be reached, the question waits for a later
launch.

*Legal basis:* consent (art. 6.1.a), given for that report.

### 4.4 Whatever you send yourself

Version control pushes to a repository **you** configured, using credentials
**you** supplied, which the application stores in your operating system's own
credential store (Keychain, DPAPI or libsecret) and never transmits anywhere
else. Opening documentation, the support site, the survey or a bug report
hands a URL to your browser; from that point you are dealing with that site
under its own terms. **From version 1.5 every such address is a
`ripplefx.app/go/…` link**, which redirects your browser to wherever the page
lives today, so that a page can move without the application changing. The
site therefore sees that request, including its query — for a prefilled bug
report, the details in the next paragraph — in the host's request log (§4.1),
and keeps nothing else of it. *Purpose:* sending you to the page you opened.
*Legal basis:* legitimate interest in links from software already installed
continuing to work after a page moves (art. 6.1.f). *Retention:* as in
§4.1. The satisfaction survey is a form hosted by **Tally**,
opened in your browser, and the link carries which part of the application you
opened it from and the version you are running — nothing else, and nothing
about you.

A prefilled bug report carries the version, your operating system and its
version, how the application was installed, and the diagnostics summary the
application shows you before it opens the page. It is opened in your browser
for you to submit or not.

**A picture a document points at by address.** A Markdown document in a
project may reference an image by an `http` or `https` address rather than by
a file inside the project folder. When the application draws that document it
fetches the image from whoever hosts it, and that host sees the request and
the address it came from, exactly as it would had you opened the address in a
browser. **This includes a project somebody else sent you**, whose documents
you did not write: an image can be small enough to be invisible on screen and
still be a request, and the application does not ask before making it. A
picture that lives inside the project folder is read from your disk and
involves no network at all. We are not a party to either kind, and neither
reaches us.

### 4.5 Usage statistics — only if you switch them on

Usage statistics arrive in **Ripple Effect 1.5**. No build before it collects
any, and from 1.5 they are **opt-in**: asked once, with the complete list of
what would be sent in front of the question rather than behind a link, neither
answer preselected, and switchable off afterwards in the application's
settings. No content you author is ever included, and adding anything to that
list means asking again.

**What a row contains.** An event name from a closed list, the day it was sent
(in UTC, never the time of day), an installation identifier described below,
the application version, the operating system family, and a small bag of numbers
and enumerated values. No free text, no file names, no project names, no
paths, and nothing you wrote. As with any request over the internet, Supabase
sees the IP address a row arrives from; it is kept in the provider's request
logs for no more than 28 days, for security and troubleshooting, and it is
**never stored with the row**.

**No account is involved, and none is created.** Statistics are filed with the
application's own publishable key under a policy that permits filing them and
nothing else — the same arrangement a crash report uses. There is no sign-in
of any kind behind them, anonymous or otherwise, and therefore no user record
of yours on our servers that they could ever be attached to.

The part worth stating precisely is what "anonymous" does and does not mean
here, because it changes what your rights over it are.

**While it is switched on**, the application holds a random value it created
at the moment you switched it on. The identifier it sends with each event — so
that separate events can be told apart from separate installations — is
derived from that value and from your computer's own identifier, the one your
operating system keeps for the machine, by a one-way hash: it cannot be turned
back into either of them, and without the random value it cannot be derived
again. **Switching the statistics on is consent to the application reading
that machine identifier for this purpose**; it is read on your machine, used
only to compute the hash, and never sent or stored anywhere. The random value
is created on your machine, by you switching the setting on, and we never hold
a copy of the identifier other than in the rows themselves. During that period the data is
**pseudonymous, not anonymous**: the identifier is on your machine, so you can
give it to us, and while you can, you can exercise every right in §11 over the
rows carrying it — access, deletion, the lot. Write to
<legal@ripplefx.app> with the identifier, which the settings screen
shows you.

**Switching it off destroys the random value**, on your machine, permanently,
so the identifier can never be derived again.
Collection stops, and the link between you and the rows already sent is
severed in the only place it ever existed. From that moment the rows cannot be
connected to you by us, by you, or by anyone — which means there is nothing
left to delete, and equally nothing we could return, correct or hand over if
you asked. They are anonymous data, outside the scope of the GDPR by Recital
26, and they stay in the aggregate.

That is a real trade and it is stated rather than glossed: **switching off is
irreversible in both directions.** It is why the identifier is destroyed
rather than merely unused, and why switching the statistics back on later
mints a **new** identifier — you are a new installation, and nothing joins the
two.

*Purpose:* counting how many installations are in use, learning which parts of
the application are used, and whether a release made it slower or less
stable. *Legal basis:* consent (art. 6.1.a).
*Recipient:* Supabase, as our processor (§9). *Retention:* **24 months** from
receipt at most, after which rows are deleted or reduced to totals that carry
no identifier. The record that an installation was in use on a given day is
kept for **30 days**, then deleted: an installation that stops reporting
simply stops being counted.

### 4.6 Engine fixes — unless you switch them off

From version 1.5 the application can take a fix to its **engine** — the part
that plays and compiles campaigns — without a new installer. While *Engine
updates*, under *Settings ▸ Updates*, is on, which it is unless you switch it
off, it asks
`ripplefx.app` once per launch whether such a fix exists. The request carries
the engine version it is running, the milestone it belongs to, the platform
(operating system and processor type) and an identifier of the interface
between the application and its engine: **nothing about you or your projects**,
and nothing unique to your installation. If a fix exists, it is downloaded
from GitHub, through a redirect from `ripplefx.app`, checked against a
signature, and used from the next launch. A fix never changes what the
application can do; it only corrects or speeds up what it already does.

*Purpose:* keeping software already installed working correctly and securely.
*Legal basis:* legitimate interest (art. 6.1.f). *Retention:* the host's request
log, as in §4.1; GitHub sees the download under [its own privacy
statement](https://docs.github.com/site-policy/privacy-policies/github-general-privacy-statement).
Switching it off stops both requests at once.

### 4.7 News — unless you switch it off

From version 1.5.1 the application can show short news from us — a release,
a change worth knowing about, something that needs your attention — on its
home screen, without you visiting the website. While *News*, under *Settings
▸ Updates*, is on, which it is unless you switch it off, it asks
`ripplefx.app` once per launch for the current list of news. The request
carries **no identifier, no version number and nothing about you or your
projects**, and no cookie. Each item may carry a picture, and the application
fetches it from `ripplefx.app` or one of its subdomains **and from nowhere
else**: a picture any item points at elsewhere is not fetched at all. Which
items you have read is recorded on your machine only, as the date of the
newest one you opened, and is never sent.

If you accepted version 1.0 of the End-User Licence Agreement, none of this
happens until you accept version 2.0.

*Purpose:* telling the people who use the application about it, without
asking them to visit a website or to install anything. *Legal basis:*
legitimate interest in communicating with the users of software already
installed (art. 6.1.f); switching *News* off is how you object to it, and it
stops the request at once. *Retention:* the host's request log, as in §4.1.

### 4.8 A dictation model — only when you ask for one

From version 1.6 the editor can turn what you say into text (§3.1), with a
speech-recognition model that runs on your computer. It needs the model on
disk first, and the application downloads it **only when you ask**: when you
choose *Download* or *Resume* for a model under *Settings ▸ General ▸
Dictation*, or answer yes when the first launch asks whether most of your
writing is in English, in which case the download starts once that first
launch is over. Each model is about 2.3 or 2.4 GB, and you can delete it
from the same place.

The files are fetched from **Hugging Face**, at `huggingface.co` and the
storage it redirects the download to. Each request names one file of the
model, at a fixed version compiled into the application, and when a
download is resumed it says from which byte. It carries **no identifier
and nothing about you or your projects**, and no account or token is used.
Hugging Face sees the IP address and the ordinary metadata of the request,
under [its own privacy policy](https://huggingface.co/privacy). Every file is
checked against a digest compiled into the application before it is used,
so what is on your disk is exactly the model that was published.

*Purpose:* giving you the dictation you asked for. *Legal basis:* legitimate
interest in providing a function of the software at your request (art.
6.1.f); not asking for a model is how you avoid the request, and nothing
else in the application makes it. *Retention:* we receive nothing of it;
Hugging Face keeps its own logs under its policy.

## 5. Crash reports, in detail

### What is in one

- **A minidump** — a snapshot of the application at the moment it crashed:
  what each part of it was running, the libraries it had loaded, the
  processor's state and small pieces of the memory around them. It also names
  your operating system and processor, and on Linux and macOS the command line
  and environment variables the application was started with.
- **The crashed session's diagnostic files** — `session.json`,
  `metadata.json`, and the logs `editor.log`, `engine.log`, `flutter.log` and
  `platform.log`. A log is sent as its **last 2 MiB**, and the dialog tells you
  when one was cut.
- **A manifest** naming the files, their sizes, the version that sent them,
  the platform, and when the crash happened.
- **A few annotations**: the application's version, whether you were on the
  home screen or inside a project (**never which project**), an identifier for
  that run so the dump can be matched with its logs, and the build type.

### What is masked before you are even asked

Before the dialog opens, and before any project is opened in that session, the
report is rewritten to replace: your **username** and **home folder**, the
**folder of every project the application knows about**, **e-mail addresses**,
**IP addresses**, and **anything shaped like a password, a token or a key**.
The dialog offers a *Show files* button so you can open them and check.

### What masking cannot promise

**A memory snapshot may contain fragments of whatever text the application was
working on at that moment.** We say so in the dialog and we say so here,
because "everything is anonymised" is a claim nobody can verify and therefore
nobody should believe. No automatic rule can know that a passage you wrote is
private.

### Identifiers in a report

A crash report involves **no account and no name**. It does carry three
identifiers, and it is more honest to list them than to call it anonymous
without qualification:

1. **A report identifier** — a random UUID for that one report.
2. **A session identifier** — matching the dump to its own logs.
3. **A per-installation random identifier.** The crash reporter generates a
   random number the first time it runs and keeps it on your machine, so that
   crashes coming from one installation can be told apart from crashes coming
   from different ones. It is not derived from your hardware, your name or
   anything else about you, and it is not used for any other purpose. It is
   nonetheless a **stable pseudonymous identifier**. Apart from the statistics
   identifier in §4.5, which exists only while you have statistics switched
   on, it is the only one anywhere in the application.

If you never send a crash report, none of these ever leaves your machine.

*Recipient:* Supabase, as our processor (§9). *Retention:* **12 months** from
receipt, then deleted. *Your rights:* because a report carries no account and
no name, we usually cannot connect one to you — if you want a report you sent
deleted, send us its identifier, which the application's dialog shows you, and
we will delete it.

## 6. The session log stays on your machine

The application keeps a log of each session in your operating system's
application-support directory. It records what the application did, with your
username, home folder, project locations, e-mail addresses, IP addresses and
anything shaped like a key **replaced before anything is written to disk**.
Times are recorded in UTC, never your local time, so that a log does not carry
a rough location.

**There is no uploader for it.** A session in which nothing went wrong is
deleted automatically on the next launch; the rest are kept to a ceiling of
**twenty sessions and thirty days**. The only way a session log reaches us is
inside a crash report you chose to send.

It records when one of your answers in §3.1 changes — which permission, from
what to what — and never anything a permission let the application read.

The application also records, on your machine only, **when it was last
running** — one timestamp, overwritten each time — which is how the next
launch can tell that the previous one ended in a crash.

## 7. The website

**Statistics.** We use **Vercel Web Analytics**, which sets no cookie and
stores nothing on your device. Visitors are distinguished by a hash derived
from the request itself, which is discarded after 24 hours and cannot be
reversed or used to recognise you on another day or another site. A recorded
view may contain the time, the path, the referring page, filtered query
parameters, an approximate location no finer than a city, your operating
system and browser and their versions, and whether the device is a phone, a
tablet or a desktop. *Legal basis:* legitimate interest in understanding
whether the site works (art. 6.1.f).

**Downloads.** The installers are in a private repository. The site holds a
read-only token, server-side, so it can ask GitHub what the current release is
and where its files are; the token never reaches your browser. The download
itself is a redirect — GitHub serves the bytes, so that request is between you
and GitHub under their privacy statement, and their per-file tally is the only
download figure anyone here has. Builds of the engine (§4.6) are published in
a public repository on GitHub and are downloaded the same way.

**Cookies.** The site sets only cookies that are technically necessary,
**except** the single first-party cookie belonging to the Buy Me a Coffee
button, which is set only if you accept it in the banner. Decline and the
script is never requested at all. You can change your mind on the
[privacy page](https://ripplefx.app/privacy) at any time. *Legal basis:*
consent (art. 6.1.a). There are no advertising, profiling or cross-site
tracking cookies, no tag manager, no session recorder and no heat map.

**Fonts** are served from the site itself; no request is made to a font
provider.

**Forms.** The waiting list and the satisfaction survey are hosted by
**Tally**. What you type goes to Tally, not into a database of ours. *Legal
basis:* consent (art. 6.1.a).

## 10. Transfers outside the European Union

Some of these providers process data outside the European Economic Area, and
the basis for the transfer is not the same one for all of them.

**The United Kingdom** is where our Supabase project runs, so a crash report
you choose to send — and the usage statistics, if you switch them on — are
processed there. The United Kingdom is covered by the European Commission's
**adequacy decisions of 19 December 2025**, which renewed its 2021 decisions
until **27 December 2031**: under article 45, a transfer to a country the
Commission has found adequate needs no further safeguard. Standard Contractual
Clauses are in our agreement with the provider in any case, so that the
transfer still has a basis if that adequacy is ever withdrawn, suspended or
allowed to lapse.

**The United States** is where the remaining providers outside the Union
process. For the three that process data **on our behalf**, the transfer rests
on:

- **Vercel** and **Resend** — the **EU–US Data Privacy Framework**, the
  Commission's adequacy decision of 10 July 2023 (art. 45), under which each
  is certified. A certification is public, and can be checked, on the
  [Data Privacy Framework list](https://www.dataprivacyframework.gov/list).
- **Forward Email** — the Commission's **Standard Contractual Clauses**
  (Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/914, art. 46), which its data-processing
  agreement incorporates.

**GitHub, Hugging Face and Polar do not process data on our behalf.** What
reaches them is a request your own machine or browser makes to them — a
download, a picture — or a payment you make with them, and each is responsible
for it under its own privacy notice, which says how it safeguards a transfer.

**A copy of the safeguards.** The Data Privacy Framework certifications are
public at the address above, and the Standard Contractual Clauses are the
Commission's published text. To obtain a copy of the clauses as they stand in
our agreement with Supabase or Forward Email, write to
<legal@ripplefx.app>; parts that are commercially confidential may be left
out, and nothing about how your data is protected will be.

Because a provider's infrastructure can abstract the region a particular
operation runs in, the [sub-processors list](/subprocessors/) names each
provider's stated processing location and is what is kept current; their own
notices carry the detail beyond that.

## 11. Your rights

### Your right to object

**You may object at any time**, on grounds relating to your particular
situation, to any processing this notice bases on legitimate interest (art.
6.1.f): the update check, the engine-fix check, the list of contributors, the
news, the redirects and the website's logs (art. 21(1)). Where the processing

## 14. Changes to this notice

This notice is versioned `major.minor`. A **minor** version changes the
wording. A **major** version changes what is processed, on what basis or for
how long; it is published, with notice on the website and in the application,
**before the release that begins the processing it describes**.

**This notice carries no effective date, and that is deliberate.** You do not
agree to a privacy notice — you are told something, and a notice is tied to
the processing it describes rather than to a day of the month. Each section
here says which release starts what it covers, and until that release there is
nothing for it to describe.

**It only ever looks forward.** Being informed is something that happens
*before* data is collected: arts. 13 and 14 oblige us to tell you at that
point, and no later wording can retroactively have told you anything. So a new
version of this notice never recasts processing that has already happened as
though it had been disclosed. If something new is to be processed, this notice
says so first, naming the release. And where the basis is your **consent**, a
consent you gave to an older list of what is sent counts as no consent at all:
the application treats it as switched off and asks you again.

**A version that is not yet in force may be corrected.** Until a version
describes processing that has actually begun, it binds nothing and informs
nobody, so a mistake in it is corrected where it stands. Once it is in force,
the text at its permanent address never changes again.

Every version that has ever been in force stays published at its own permanent
address. This one is at <https://legal.ripplefx.app/v/privacy-3.0/>.
